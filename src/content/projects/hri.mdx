### The Question

What kind of conditions promote helping a robot in need — and how does a robot's *perceived* level of autonomy change that behavior? Do people have the capacity to feel empathy toward a machine whose only purpose is to serve them?

These were the questions driving our Human-Robot Interaction final project at Carnegie Mellon. We designed a full experiment around **Janitobot**: a custodial robot whose job was to clean spills on the CMU campus floor.

### Literature Review

We grounded the experiment in prior research on **trust, empathy, and perceived reliability** in robotics. The central tension: humans are more willing to help other humans than machines, but robots that appear to have limited agency might trigger a different social response.

### Robot & Interaction Design

We settled on a custodial robot to ground the experiment in a believable, everyday scenario. Janitobot's job was to vacuum up a spill (M&Ms and Skittles) in its target area. It would fail mid-task and make a verbal appeal for help to whoever passed by.

<ProcessImage src="/projects/hri/robot.jpg" alt="CAD render of Janitobot showing vacuum hose arm connected to a chest basket, with a tablet at the neck" caption="CAD render by Yooyoung Ko. The vacuum hose connects to a chest basket; the iPad at the neck displays either a human operator, his office, or the words 'I am an autonomous robot.'" />

We used **Burke's Dramatic Pentad** — a framework from rhetoric — to design the full staged interaction: who the robot was, what it was doing, where it was doing it, how it behaved, and why. This kept the encounter feeling real rather than theatrical.

<ProcessImage src="/projects/hri/pentagram.jpg" alt="Burke's Dramatic Pentad diagram applied to the robot interaction staging" caption="Burke's Dramatic Pentad applied to the Janitobot encounter — ensuring the scene read as a credible, real-world event." />

### Experiment: Three Conditions

We ran an uncontrolled field experiment in a low-traffic area of campus, varying Janitobot's **perceived level of autonomy** across three conditions:

- **Operator Present** — Robot's human controller was clearly visible nearby
- **Semi-Autonomous** — Controller perceived to be absent, robot appeared to act semi-independently
- **Fully Autonomous** — Robot appeared to act entirely on its own

The robot made the same verbal appeal in all three conditions. We observed whether passers-by stopped and helped.

### Findings

Participants were significantly more likely to help when the robot appeared to have a human operator involved — suggesting that perceived human accountability, not robot autonomy alone, drives helping behavior. The fully autonomous condition generated the least help.
